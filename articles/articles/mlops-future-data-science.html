Why Your ML Models Aren’t Making It to Production

MLOps
Data Science
Machine Learning Engineering
The operational gap between prototype and production is where most AI investments go to die
Published

2026-01-15

The 85% Problem

Most machine learning projects fail. Not because the algorithms don’t work, but because organizations can’t operationalize them. A 2024 analysis found that 85% of ML initiatives never deliver business value in production (Galileo), a staggering figure for technology that many companies are betting their futures and resources on.

The pattern is familiar to anyone who’s worked in data science. A team spends months developing a model. The metrics look perfect, the stakeholders are excited, and six months from now everyone will have quietly moved on, the model still trapped in a Jupyter notebook. The graveyard of promising models is vast.

This isn’t a talent problem or a tools problem. It’s an infrastructure problem. The discipline that addresses it, MLOps, has become the difference between organizations that extract value from ML and those that don’t.

The Problem

Somewhere in your organization, a data scientist is building a model that will never see the light of day. The metrics look perfect, the stakeholders are excited, and six months from now everyone will have quietly moved on, the model still trapped in a Jupyter notebook. This is not a failure of machine learning. It is a failure of infrastructure, of process, and the complexities between “it works on my laptop” and “it works at scale, reliably, for millions of users.”

Consider what happens when models do make it to production. An e-commerce company spends months developing a recommendation system that achieves strong performance on test sets and shows promising results in A/B testing, but when deployed, the model’s performance degrades significantly within weeks. The training data might have been collected during a holiday season, causing the model to learn patterns specific to that period that don’t generalize to normal shopping behavior. Without proper monitoring and drift detection, this degradation goes unnoticed until customer complaints about irrelevant recommendations start piling up. This scenario, documented in case studies from companies like Zalando and Netflix, illustrates how operational constraints often cause more production failures than algorithmic problems themselves (Zalando case study, Netflix RecSysOps).

When models do see the light of day, they often fail in ways that take months to notice. An e-commerce company I spoke with spent the better part of a year developing a recommendation system. Test performance was strong. A/B results looked promising. Within weeks of deployment, the whole thing started falling apart. The culprit was banal: the training data had been collected during a holiday season, and the model had learned shopping patterns that evaporated once normal life resumed. Without drift detection, nobody noticed until customer complaints about bizarre recommendations reached a critical mass. Case studies from Zalando and Netflix tell similar stories. Operational failures, it turns out, cause more production problems than algorithmic ones (Zalando case study, Netflix RecSysOps).

What Makes ML Different

You could describe MLOps as DevOps for machine learning, but that undersells the strangeness of what we’re dealing with. Traditional software is, in a sense, honest. You write code, you test it, you deploy it, and it does what you told it to do. Machine learning systems are different. They are shaped by data in ways that make them unpredictable, even to the people who built them.

The same code can produce wildly different outputs as data distributions shift. A model that worked brilliantly yesterday might fail tomorrow, and nothing in the codebase will have changed. Performance degrades as the real world drifts away from the world captured in training data. Random seeds, hardware quirks, and dependency versions conspire to make reproducibility a constant struggle. And in regulated industries, you need explainability and audit trails that traditional software never demanded.

The seminal paper on this subject, “Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems,” put it starkly: the ML code itself represents only a tiny fraction of a production system’s complexity. The rest is data collection, feature extraction, configuration, serving infrastructure, and monitoring. The model is the easy part (Sculley et al., 2015).

Uber learned this the hard way with their ETA predictions. Models trained on San Francisco traffic failed catastrophically in Mumbai. Models that worked in January degraded by March as patterns shifted. Their solution was to build automated pipelines that monitor accuracy and trigger retraining without human intervention, a system that required feature stores, model registries, and testing infrastructure that rivals anything in traditional software engineering (Uber Michelangelo, DeepETA).

Why It Matters Now

For years, machine learning lived in the back office. It powered internal analytics, batch processing, things that happened overnight while everyone slept. That era is over. ChatGPT handles hundreds of millions of conversations every week. Voice assistants respond in real time. Recommendation algorithms shape what billions of people see, read, and buy. Search engines have started generating answers instead of just pointing to them.

When machine learning moves from analytics to product, everything changes. A notebook model can take hours to run. A consumer product must respond in milliseconds. A notebook model can fail quietly. A consumer product that fails visibly destroys trust. A notebook model can be retrained whenever someone gets around to it. A consumer product must adapt continuously without going dark.

Netflix processes billions of interactions daily while simultaneously retraining the models that power recommendations. This requires coordinating hundreds of models across multiple services, a feat of orchestration that would have seemed absurd a decade ago (Netflix Maestro, Netflix foundation model).

Large language models have made all of this more urgent. They power chatbots and coding assistants and creative tools, but they also introduce failure modes we’re only beginning to understand: hallucinations, prompt injection attacks, outputs that seem confident but are completely wrong. LLMOps, as practitioners have started calling it, focuses less on training from scratch and more on prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and figuring out how to deploy responsibly (OpenAI Realtime API).

Regulators have noticed. The EU AI Act now requires organizations to demonstrate model governance, explainability, and auditability. Research suggests that 85% of ML projects fail to deliver business value in production, often because of silent failures, distribution drift, and pipeline breaks that nobody catches until real damage is done (ML Observability Framework). In consumer-facing applications, these failures aren’t silent at all. Users notice. Revenue suffers.

What You Actually Need

A startup with one model has different needs than a bank running hundreds of risk models. Still, certain components show up everywhere.

Version control needs to extend beyond code. Models, data, configurations, and experiments all need versioning because the relationships between them determine how models behave. Tools like DVC, MLflow, and Weights & Biases can track the full lineage from raw data to deployment. This becomes critical when you need to understand, months later, why a model made a particular decision.

Reproducible environments matter more than people expect. A model trained with TensorFlow 2.8 might produce different outputs when deployed with TensorFlow 2.9, even with identical weights. Changes in numerical precision and optimization algorithms can shift results in subtle ways. Strict containerization and pinned dependencies are the only reliable defense.

Testing has to happen at multiple levels because ML systems fail in ways traditional software doesn’t. You need unit tests for data processing, integration tests for pipelines, validation tests for data quality and schema drift, performance tests against accuracy thresholds, and infrastructure tests for deployment configurations.

Monitoring splits into three domains. System monitoring tracks the usual suspects: CPU, memory, latency. Data monitoring watches feature distributions, missing values, schema changes. Model monitoring tracks prediction distributions, accuracy over time, and drift. Tools like Evidently AI and WhyLabs can help, though custom dashboards on Prometheus and Grafana remain common.

Airbnb discovered something subtle about their pricing models: individual predictions looked reasonable, but in aggregate, the system was pricing every property in certain neighborhoods too high during peak periods. Catching this required monitoring not just individual outputs but aggregate patterns and business metrics (Airbnb pricing).

A model registry centralizes metadata, performance metrics, and approval workflows, making it possible to roll back when something goes wrong. Feature stores solve the problem of teams reimplementing the same feature engineering logic across projects, introducing inconsistencies that are maddening to debug. Uber’s Palette system serves features for both real-time inference and training, ensuring that what the model learned matches what it sees in production (Uber Palette).

The Hard Part

None of this is primarily a technology problem. The hardest part is organizational.

Data scientists, ML engineers, DevOps engineers, and product managers need to work together in ways that traditional corporate structures actively discourage. Silos that took years to build have to come down. The culture has to shift from “move fast and break things” toward something more like “move deliberately and watch everything.”

MLOps demands a blend of data science, software engineering, and infrastructure expertise that few individuals possess. Some organizations have responded by creating dedicated ML platform teams that build tooling and infrastructure, freeing data scientists to focus on modeling while platform engineers handle operational complexity.

Perhaps the deepest challenge is defining what success actually means. Traditional software metrics like uptime and latency don’t capture what matters for ML systems. A recommendation engine might boost click-through rates while trapping users in filter bubbles. A fraud detection system might achieve high accuracy while flagging so many legitimate transactions that customers abandon ship. These tradeoffs require judgment that spans both technical and business domains, and organizations that can’t make those judgments well tend to build systems that optimize for the wrong things.

Getting Started

If you’re starting from scratch, resist the urge to build everything at once. Version your models and data first. Even basic versioning provides enormous value and creates the foundation for everything else. Add monitoring next, starting simple: prediction distributions, latency, error rates. Pick one manual process and automate it, whether that’s deployment or retraining or testing. Learn from that before expanding.

The organizations that succeed tend to start with their biggest pain points rather than with some idealized architecture. MLOps is iterative. You build what you need, discover new problems, and build again.

Why This Matters

MLOps is no longer optional. As machine learning moves from research curiosity to production infrastructure, organizations that invest in operational excellence will pull ahead. More importantly, robust operations are becoming a prerequisite for responsible deployment.

The models we build now influence loan approvals, medical diagnoses, hiring decisions, and resource allocation at a scale that would have been unimaginable a decade ago. Deploying these systems without proper monitoring, governance, and operational discipline isn’t just bad engineering. It is, in a meaningful sense, reckless.

The future of this field isn’t just about building better models. It’s about building models that work reliably, transparently, and ethically once they leave the notebook. MLOps is the bridge that makes that possible.

The Failure Rate Nobody Talks About

A recent analysis found that 85% of machine learning projects never deliver business value in production (Galileo). That number should be shocking, but if you’ve spent time in the industry, it probably isn’t. You’ve seen the pattern: a team spends months on a model, the accuracy metrics look strong, leadership gets excited, and then the project stalls somewhere between “working prototype” and “deployed system.” Or it launches, degrades quietly, and gets shut down a year later when someone finally audits it.

The problem isn’t that machine learning doesn’t work. The problem is that most organizations have no idea how to run it in production. They’ve hired data scientists, bought cloud compute, maybe even acquired some promising startups. What they haven’t done is build the operational infrastructure that turns experimental models into reliable systems. The industry has started calling this infrastructure MLOps, a name that doesn’t do justice to how important it is.

Where Things Go Wrong

The most common failure isn’t a bad algorithm. It’s a good model that slowly stops working because nobody’s watching it.

Machine learning models are trained on historical data, but the world doesn’t stay still. Customer preferences shift. Competitors change their pricing. Fraud patterns evolve. A recommendation model trained on last year’s behavior might be confidently suggesting products that nobody wants anymore. Zalando documented this happening to their own systems; accuracy degraded substantially before anyone noticed, and by then the user experience damage was done (Zalando).

The second class of failures is more subtle. During training, a model learns from features computed by one pipeline. In production, those same features get computed by a different system, often built by a different team. Small inconsistencies creep in. A timestamp gets parsed differently. A null value gets handled differently. The model sees data that doesn’t quite match what it learned from, and its predictions suffer. Uber built their entire Michelangelo platform largely to eliminate this problem, which tells you how serious it is (Uber).

Then there’s the reproducibility problem, which tends to bite at the worst times. Someone needs to retrain a model that’s been running for a year. The original data scientist has left. The notebook they used references data that’s been moved, dependencies that have been updated, and parameters that were tweaked by hand and never recorded. Reconstructing the model becomes archaeology. I’ve watched teams spend weeks trying to reproduce results that took days to generate originally.

What Operational Maturity Looks Like

The organizations that consistently get value from ML have built infrastructure that most companies lack.

They version everything, not just code. Training data, model weights, feature definitions, hyperparameters, environment configurations. The goal is to answer a simple question: what exactly produced this prediction? If you can’t reconstruct a model’s lineage on demand, you can’t audit decisions, debug production issues, or reliably retrain. Tools like DVC and MLflow help, but plenty of organizations still haven’t adopted them systematically (DVC, MLflow).

They’ve centralized feature management. Once you’re running more than a handful of models, having each team compute its own features becomes a liability. Definitions diverge. Training and serving get out of sync. Bugs become nearly impossible to trace. Feature stores provide a single source of truth, and the good ones serve features with low enough latency for real-time inference while maintaining exact parity with offline training. Uber’s Palette handles millions of lookups per second this way (Palette).

They monitor at multiple levels. Traditional application monitoring, the kind that tracks latency and error rates, isn’t enough. You also need to track the distribution of predictions, the drift in input features, and actual model accuracy when ground truth becomes available. Airbnb learned this when they discovered their pricing models were systematically overpricing certain neighborhoods. Individual predictions looked reasonable; only business-level monitoring revealed the aggregate problem (Airbnb).

The Part That Isn’t About Technology

The tools exist. The harder problem is organizational.

Data scientists and engineers have different objectives. Data scientists want to maximize model performance. Engineers want systems that are reliable, maintainable, and don’t page them at 3am. These goals aren’t always compatible, and without deliberate effort to align them, the handoff from research to production becomes adversarial. I’ve seen promising models die in that gap because neither side felt ownership of getting them across.

Some companies have addressed this by creating dedicated platform teams that build ML infrastructure for everyone else. Others have invested in a hybrid role, the ML engineer, who can work on both sides of the divide. What doesn’t work is treating production deployment as someone else’s problem.

Large Language Models Have Made This Urgent

LLMs compress timelines and raise stakes. Organizations expect to deploy AI capabilities in weeks now, not quarters. The models power customer-facing products with failure modes we’re still learning to handle: hallucinations, prompt injection, outputs that sound authoritative but are wrong. They require specialized infrastructure for inference at scale.

The operational challenges differ from traditional ML. There’s less focus on training and more on prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and figuring out how to evaluate outputs that don’t have clean right-or-wrong answers. But the core lesson is the same: the gap between “working demo” and “production system” is where most initiatives fail.

The Compounding Advantage

The 85% failure rate isn’t distributed evenly. Organizations with mature operations deploy models faster, catch problems earlier, and build institutional knowledge that compounds. Those without keep investing in data science while watching projects stall.

The gap will widen. As ML becomes infrastructure rather than experiment, the ability to operate it reliably becomes the bottleneck. Companies that solve MLOps will be able to use AI. Everyone else will keep running pilots.
